# The Cree partnership — the track's fourth language

## The model (shared with Cherokee, Navajo, and Lakota)

This document extends the partnership track
(`docs/i18n-partnership-cherokee.md`, argued in full there) to
Cree — the Algonquian family's entry in the
one-language-per-family plan, and the language whose question
started the whole track. In brief:

**Cree speakers translate, we supply all tooling, and nothing
ships before their review.** The AI-fleet pipeline behind the
seventeen shipped languages would not be honest for a
low-resource language, and language authority rests with
speakers. The offer is the standing one, stated plainly rather
than assumed: the whole technical burden on us; no deadline, any
pace; credit and review authority theirs, including the words
the Settings language card uses and unshipping on request;
compensation discussed honestly in the first conversation;
licensing and materials ownership discussed before work starts,
in writing.

## What is different about Cree

- **Cree is a continuum, not a standard** — Plains, Woods,
  Swampy, Moose, and East Cree, each with its own communities and
  institutions. So this track does not ask "will you translate
  Cree?" but "would YOUR Cree belong here?" — and the plan holds
  room for more than one answer: the app's registry is per-tag,
  and several dialects can ship over time as separate entries.
  Whichever community engages first sets the first path.
- **The technical profile differs by dialect** (verified against
  our runtime, `docs/i18n-glossary/cr.md`): Swampy Cree is a
  first-class CLDR/ICU locale — native plural rules and syllabics
  month names free at every date call site — while Plains, Woods,
  Moose, and East Cree are absent from CLDR and ride our proven
  no-ICU pattern. Both paths are shipped precedent; neither costs
  partners anything.
- **Two living writing systems** — syllabics and Standard Roman
  Orthography — used differently across communities. Which one an
  interface speaks (or which is primary) is the second-order
  partner decision, and the rendering ground for both is verified
  and solid.
- **Language-technology partnership is already a Cree
  tradition.** The morphologically intelligent Plains Cree
  dictionary built with Maskwacîs educators, and East Cree's
  long-running community web-dictionary project, are exactly the
  community-partnership model in language technology. We would be
  the newest, smallest entrant in an existing practice — and our
  tooling can interoperate with that work rather than duplicate
  it.

## Who to contact

Community and institutional programs across the continuum; the
person sending this does not live on Cree territory, and the note
below is written accordingly. Which door comes first is the
sender's judgment — each column of the continuum has its own:

- **Cree School Board / Commission scolaire crie** (Eeyou
  Istchee, Quebec) — East Cree; runs Cree-language education for
  the Cree Nation of Eeyou Istchee, alongside the **Cree Nation
  Government**.
- **First Nations University of Canada** (Regina) — Plains Cree
  degree programs and teacher education.
- **University nuhelot'įne thaiyots'į nistameyimâkanak Blue
  Quills** (St. Paul, Alberta) — the First Nations-governed
  institution with nêhiyawêwin immersion programming.
- **Maskwacîs education institutions** (Alberta) — the community
  whose partnership built the Plains Cree language-technology
  tools.
- Northern school divisions and immersion programs in
  Saskatchewan and Manitoba for Woods and Swampy Cree, as doors
  open.

If several communities engage — or the Cherokee, Navajo, and
Lakota tracks have by then — each should know of the others;
lessons flow both ways and the scaffolds cross-reference.

## Draft outreach note (personalize before sending)

> Subject: An invitation — a mutual-aid app's interface in Cree,
> on your terms
>
> Tânisi — my name is [name]. I help maintain Understoria, a
> free, open-source mutual-aid platform: neighbors exchange help
> hour-for-hour, communities run their own servers, there are no
> ads, no tracking, and no company behind it. It speaks seventeen
> languages today, and we have begun a different kind of
> translation track for Indigenous languages — the same
> invitation has gone to Cherokee, Navajo, and Lakota language
> programs.
>
> I'm writing to ask whether an interface in your Cree — in your
> dialect, in the writing system your community uses — is
> something your program would find worthwhile. The offer is the
> opposite of the usual one: we are not asking permission to
> machine-translate the language, and we won't do that. The
> technical groundwork is done for both syllabics and roman
> orthography, we've documented the translation decisions that
> proved hard in seventeen other languages, and the ask is
> whether Cree speakers — your staff, your students, whoever
> you'd choose — would want to do the actual translation, at
> whatever pace works, with review authority over every word,
> credit however you want it, ownership of the language materials
> settled in writing first, and compensation discussed honestly
> up front. Nothing ships until you say it's right; there is no
> deadline on our side; and if a different dialect's community is
> the better fit, we'd welcome the redirection — the door is open
> to more than one Cree.
>
> If interface localization has any place in your language work —
> or if it doesn't, but you'd point me somewhere better — I'd be
> glad for a short conversation. Kinanâskomitin for reading this,
> and for the work you do.
>
> [name, contact, link to the project]
